# Landing Page Copywriting

A method for writing landing pages that answer the visitor's questions in the order they ask them. It combines awareness-level structure (after Eugene Schwartz's stages of awareness), headline formulas used to generate options, and a proof discipline that never invents evidence.

## When to use

- Building a landing page for a campaign, product, feature, or lead magnet.
- Rewriting a page with high bounce or low conversion where diagnosis points at messaging, clarity, or proof.
- Producing copy variants for an A/B test.

Not for brand voice policing (use a voice review), for deciding the positioning itself (do positioning first), or for SEO-led long-form articles, which have different structure.

## Inputs

- **Offer** — what the visitor gets and what they must do (sign up, book, buy, download). Required.
- **Audience and traffic source** — who arrives and from where (search ad for a specific query, cold social ad, email to customers). Required; it sets awareness level.
- **Positioning or messaging** — value themes, differentiators, primary alternative. Recommended.
- **Proof inventory** — real testimonials with permission, case study results, customer counts, ratings, certifications, press. Required to place proof; missing proof is flagged.
- **Objections** — from sales, support, surveys. Recommended.
- **Constraints** — voice guide, legal requirements, layout or length limits.

## Procedure

1. **Diagnose awareness.** Classify the dominant visitor: unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or most aware. A search ad for "invoice software for freelancers" brings solution-aware visitors; a cold social ad brings problem-aware or unaware visitors. Lower awareness needs more problem framing before the product appears; higher awareness wants the offer and proof immediately.
2. **Match the promise.** Write down the exact promise of the ad, email, or link that brings visitors. The headline must continue that promise in recognisable words; message mismatch is a common cause of bounce.
3. **Generate headlines.** Produce 8-12 options using several formulas, then pick 3: outcome plus timeframe or effort ("Send your first invoice in two minutes"); problem-agitate ("Still chasing late payments by email?"); specific audience ("Invoicing for freelancers who hate admin"); differentiator versus alternative ("Everything your spreadsheet does, plus automatic reminders"); question; and "how to" promise. Prefer specific over clever. Add a subheadline that says what the product is and how it delivers the promise.
4. **Order the sections.** A default for solution-aware traffic: hero (headline, subheadline, primary CTA, supporting visual), proof bar, problem and stakes, how it works (three steps), benefits (outcome-first, feature as support), detailed proof (case study or testimonials matched to benefits), objection handling (FAQ), offer recap and CTA. For unaware traffic, expand problem framing before introducing the product; for most-aware traffic, shorten to offer, proof, and CTA.
5. **Write benefits from features.** For each feature, write the outcome it creates for this audience, then the feature as the reason to believe. Use concrete specifics (numbers, time, named tasks) only where they are true and supplied.
6. **Place proof where doubt occurs.** Put a short proof element next to every major claim: a testimonial that echoes the claim, a result, a logo set. Use only proof from the inventory. Where proof is missing, insert a clearly marked placeholder such as [PROOF NEEDED: customer result for time saved] rather than inventing one.
7. **Handle objections.** Turn the top objections into FAQ entries or inline reassurance near the CTA (price, switching effort, security, contract terms, "will it work for my case").
8. **Write the CTA.** One primary action, repeated; button text that describes what happens ("Start my free trial", "Get the checklist"), with friction reducers adjacent (no card required, cancel anytime) only if true.
9. **Edit for scan and clarity.** Visitors skim: each section should make sense from its heading alone. Cut adjectives that don't carry information, remove hedges, and keep paragraphs short. Read the page from the perspective of the traffic source promise.
10. **Prepare variants.** If testing, vary one big idea (headline angle, page length, proof-first versus problem-first), not wording, and hand the variants to test design.

## Expected output

```markdown
# Landing Page Copy: <page>
**Audience/awareness:** ... · **Traffic promise:** ... · **Primary CTA:** ...

## Headline options
1. ... (formula) 2. ... 3. ...  Recommended: # because ...

## Page copy by section
### Hero — headline, subheadline, CTA, visual note
### Proof bar
### Problem
### How it works
### Benefits
### Proof (with [PROOF NEEDED] markers)
### FAQ / objections
### Final CTA

## Claims to verify
| Claim | Source needed | Status |

## Test variants (optional)
```

## Constraints and limitations

- Never invent testimonials, ratings, customer counts, results, or endorsements.
- Superlatives and comparative claims ("best", "fastest", "cheaper than X") need substantiation and often legal review.
- Copy cannot compensate for a weak offer or wrong audience.
- Formulas generate options; they do not guarantee performance, so test where traffic allows.

## Examples

Traffic: search ad "automatic invoice reminders for freelancers". Awareness: solution-aware. Headline options: "Get paid on time without chasing clients" (outcome), "Automatic invoice reminders for freelancers" (continues the query), "Stop writing awkward payment-reminder emails" (problem). Recommended: the second as headline because it matches the query exactly, with the first as subheadline. Proof next to the reminders benefit: [PROOF NEEDED: customer quote about payment speed]. Objection near CTA: "Will reminders annoy my clients?" answered with the editable tone settings, if the product supports it.
