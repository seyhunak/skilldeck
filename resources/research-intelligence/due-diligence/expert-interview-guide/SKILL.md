# Expert Interview Guide

## When to use

Use this skill when preparing a 30-60 minute conversation with someone who has specialist knowledge: a former industry executive via an expert network, a customer reference for a vendor or acquisition target, a channel partner, or a technical specialist. It fits due diligence, market research, vendor evaluation, and strategy work.

It is not for user-research interviews about product usability (those need a different, behaviour-focused script) or for surveys. It does not replace the compliance policies of the expert network or the user's organization; it supports them.

## Inputs

- The decision the research feeds and the three to six hypotheses or open questions to test.
- The expert's profile: role, employer history, how recently they left, and their relationship to the subject.
- What is already known from desk research, so the call does not waste time on public facts.
- Call length and any compliance rules from the user's organization or the network.

## Procedure

1. **Write hypotheses, not topics.** Convert each research question into a falsifiable statement, such as "Mid-size hospitals renew this software mainly because switching costs are high, not because of satisfaction." Rank them; the top two get the most time.
2. **Check fit and conflicts.** Confirm the expert can speak to each hypothesis from direct experience. Note conflicts: current employees of the target or its competitors, people bound by confidentiality on the topic, or anyone who may hold material non-public information about a listed company. Escalate concerns before scheduling.
3. **Open with compliance and context.** Draft a short opening that states the purpose in general terms, reminds the expert not to share confidential information of current or former employers or any material non-public information, and confirms they can decline any question.
4. **Structure the funnel.** Order the guide from broad to specific: the expert's background and vantage point (2-3 minutes), the landscape in their own words, the specific hypotheses, quantification, and finally a forward-looking question and "what should I have asked?".
5. **Write neutral, open questions.** Use "how", "what", "walk me through", and "tell me about the last time" forms. Remove leading phrasing ("Wouldn't you agree that...?"), embedded assumptions ("Why is Vendor X so expensive?"), and double-barrelled questions that ask two things at once. Ask about specific past events rather than general opinions or hypotheticals.
6. **Add probes and quantifiers.** Under each main question add follow-ups: "What makes you say that?", "Can you give an example?", "Roughly what share of cases?", "Compared with what?", "How would that have looked three years ago?". Ask for ranges rather than point numbers and note the basis of each number.
7. **Plan for disconfirmation.** Include at least one question per hypothesis designed to surface evidence against it, for example asking for cases where customers did switch despite high switching costs.
8. **Time-box.** Allocate minutes per section and mark optional questions to drop if time runs short.
9. **Prepare the note template** so calls can be compared: hypothesis-by-hypothesis evidence, quotes, numbers with basis, confidence, and follow-ups.

## Expected output

```markdown
# Interview Guide: <topic> - <expert profile>
**Decision:** ... · **Length:** 45 min · **Hypotheses:** H1..Hn (ranked)

## Opening (2 min) - compliance reminder and purpose
## Background (3 min)
## Landscape (8 min)
Q1 ... Probes: ...
## Hypothesis H1 (12 min)
Q ... Probes ... Disconfirming question ...
## Hypothesis H2 (10 min)
## Quantification (5 min)
## Close (5 min) - "What did I not ask?" / who else to speak to

## Note template
| Hypothesis | Supports / contradicts / neutral | Evidence or quote | Numbers and basis | Confidence |
```

## Constraints and limitations

- Experts generalise from their own experience; one call is an anecdote, several consistent calls are a pattern. Plan the number of calls accordingly.
- Former employees' knowledge ages quickly; weight recency.
- Do not ask for confidential documents, customer lists, non-public financials, or pricing terms under NDA, and stop the line of questioning if the expert volunteers them.
- Do not use the guide to pretext or misrepresent who is asking or why.
- Interview notes may contain personal data; store and share them according to the user's policy.

## Examples

**Leading:** "Customers hate Vendor X's support, right?"
**Neutral:** "Tell me about the last time your team needed help from the vendor. What happened, and how was it resolved?"

**Double-barrelled:** "How do pricing and integration affect renewals?"
**Split:** "What were the main reasons behind your last renewal decision?" then probe pricing and integration separately if they do not come up.

**Unquantified:** "Is switching expensive?"
**Quantified:** "Walk me through the last migration you saw. Roughly how many months and people did it take, and what did that cost compare to the annual licence?"
