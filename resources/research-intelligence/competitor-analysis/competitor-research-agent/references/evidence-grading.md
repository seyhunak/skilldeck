# Evidence grading for competitor claims

This scheme adapts the two-axis source/information grading long used in intelligence analysis (often called the Admiralty or NATO system). The two axes are scored independently: a reliable publisher can still print a wrong number, and an unknown blog can be right.

## Source reliability

| Grade | Meaning | Typical competitor-research examples |
|---|---|---|
| A | Completely reliable | Regulatory filings, the competitor's own pricing page or documentation for facts about itself |
| B | Usually reliable | Established trade press with named reporters, audited annual reports, official marketplace listings |
| C | Fairly reliable | Review platforms with verified reviewers, conference talks by competitor staff |
| D | Not usually reliable | Anonymous forum posts, aggregator sites with unsourced figures |
| E | Unreliable | Content with a clear undisclosed agenda, known inaccurate sites |
| F | Cannot be judged | New or unidentifiable source |

## Information credibility

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Confirmed by an independent source |
| 2 | Probably true; consistent with other evidence |
| 3 | Possibly true; not contradicted, not confirmed |
| 4 | Doubtful; inconsistent with other evidence |
| 5 | Improbable; contradicted by stronger evidence |
| 6 | Cannot be judged |

## Practical rules

- A competitor's statements about itself are A for what it offers and how it describes itself, but its claims about market leadership, customer counts, or performance are self-interested; grade the information axis separately (often 3 until confirmed).
- Two articles repeating the same press release are one source, not two.
- Grade the claim, not the document: one article can contain a B2 fact and a B4 speculation.
- Only claims graded at least B2 or A3 should drive a recommendation on their own; anything weaker should be presented as a lead to verify.
- These thresholds are heuristics; state the grade and let the reader judge.
