# Evidence-Based SWOT

## When to use

Use this skill for strategy reviews, competitor assessments, market-entry decisions, partnership evaluations, and planning workshops where a SWOT is expected and must be defensible. It applies to an organization, a business unit, a product line, or a competitor.

Avoid SWOT when the question needs quantitative analysis (pricing, sizing, financial modelling) or a narrower tool (for example a feature comparison). SWOT is a framing device; the evidence and the TOWS actions are what make it useful.

## Inputs

- The subject (organization, product, or competitor) and the decision the SWOT informs.
- Evidence: research notes, financial or operating data, customer feedback, competitor information, market and regulatory signals.
- The comparison set: who "relative to competitors" means.
- The time horizon for opportunities and threats.

## Procedure

1. **Anchor on the decision.** Write the decision at the top (for example "whether to enter the mid-market segment in 2027"). Items unrelated to it are dropped; a SWOT that tries to cover everything decides nothing.
2. **Draft candidate items from evidence.** Read the evidence and list candidate items with a citation for each. Brainstorming is allowed, but any item without evidence is marked "hypothesis" and kept out of the main grid until supported.
3. **Apply quadrant tests.**
   - Internal or external: strengths and weaknesses are attributes the subject controls (capabilities, assets, costs, culture); opportunities and threats are external conditions (market, customers, competitors, regulation, technology). "Growing market" is an opportunity, not a strength.
   - Relative: a strength must be better than relevant competitors, not merely good. "Good customer support" is a strength only if evidence shows it outperforms alternatives buyers consider.
   - Specific: rewrite vague items ("strong brand") into evidence-bearing statements ("highest unaided awareness among UK SMB accountants in two independent surveys").
4. **Rate each item.** Score impact on the decision (high, medium, low) and evidence strength (strong, moderate, weak). For opportunities and threats, add likelihood and timing.
5. **Prioritise.** Keep the top three to five items per quadrant by impact; move the rest to an appendix.
6. **Generate TOWS options.** Pair quadrants to create concrete options:
   - SO: use strengths to capture opportunities.
   - ST: use strengths to blunt threats.
   - WO: fix weaknesses to unlock opportunities.
   - WT: defensive moves that reduce exposure where weaknesses meet threats.
   Each option names the items it combines and the first concrete step.
7. **Screen the options.** Note feasibility, cost band, and dependencies, and flag which options conflict. Recommend two or three for deeper evaluation rather than declaring a strategy.
8. **List evidence gaps** that would most change the analysis.

## Constraints and limitations

- SWOT is static and qualitative; pair it with quantitative analysis for financial decisions.
- Teams tend to over-list strengths and under-list weaknesses; the relative test counters this but needs honest comparison evidence.
- The same factor can be a strength and a weakness (for example a large installed base slowing change); record both with their evidence.
- Do not fabricate supporting data to fill a quadrant; an empty or thin quadrant is a finding.

## Examples

**Subject:** a regional grocery chain considering launching rapid home delivery.
Strength: store density within 3 km of 70% of households in its region (company store data) - a relative advantage over national rivals with fewer stores there (competitor store locators).
Weakness: no in-house app; online sales under 2% of revenue (annual report).
Opportunity: delivery demand in the region rising (survey by a reputable trade body).
Threat: two well-funded delivery platforms already operate in the main city (company announcements).
TOWS: SO - use store density for store-picked one-hour delivery in suburbs the platforms do not serve; WT - partner with an existing platform rather than build an app, limiting exposure to its weak digital capability. Both go forward for costing.
