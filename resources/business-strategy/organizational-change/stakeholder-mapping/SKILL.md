# Stakeholder Mapping

Stakeholder mapping answers three questions: who can affect or is affected by this initiative, what do they need from it, and what do we need from them. The classic power/interest grid (often attributed to Mendelow) sorts stakeholders into four engagement strategies; a commitment gap assessment adds direction by comparing each person's current stance with the stance the initiative requires.

## When to use

- At the start of a project, change programme, policy decision, or partnership.
- Before a major announcement or decision meeting.
- When support is weaker than expected and you need to know where to focus.
- Not as a substitute for an accountability matrix; for who does and approves what, use a RACI.

## Inputs

- A one-paragraph description of the initiative and its main impacts.
- Known stakeholders: individuals, roles, groups, and external parties.
- Any evidence about their positions: meeting notes, emails summarised by the user, survey results.
- The decision or support the initiative needs (budget approval, staff time, adoption, sign-off).

## Procedure

1. **Brainstorm widely, then consolidate.** List stakeholders across categories: decision-makers and funders, those whose work changes, those who must provide resources, gatekeepers (legal, security, finance, works councils), influencers without formal authority, customers and partners, and those who could block. Group people with identical interests; keep individuals separate where their influence is personal.
2. **Describe interests.** For each stakeholder, write what they care about in this context (goals, concerns, what they gain or lose) and what the initiative needs from them. Base this on evidence where possible and mark guesses.
3. **Rate power and interest.** Power = ability to influence the outcome (formal authority, control of resources, expertise, network). Interest = how much the outcome affects them or how much attention they pay. Use High/Low or a 1-5 scale with a one-line justification.
4. **Place on the grid.** High power and high interest: manage closely (involve in shaping). High power, low interest: keep satisfied (concise updates, no surprises, escalate only material issues). Low power, high interest: keep informed (regular, two-way communication; they are often early adopters or vocal critics). Low power, low interest: monitor (general updates).
5. **Assess commitment gap.** For each important stakeholder record current stance and required stance on a scale such as: actively opposed, passively opposed, neutral, supportive, champion. The biggest gaps among high-power stakeholders are the priority. Not everyone needs to be a champion; over-engaging wastes effort.
6. **Analyse relationships.** Note who influences whom. A neutral executive may be most effectively reached through a trusted peer rather than the project team.
7. **Build the engagement plan.** For each priority stakeholder define: objective (move from X to Y), key message tied to their interests, engagement owner (the person with the best relationship), channel and format, cadence, and the signal that shows progress.
8. **Set a review rhythm.** Positions change as the initiative evolves; review the map at each milestone and after significant events.

## Expected output

```markdown
## Stakeholder Register: <initiative>
| Stakeholder | Role / group | Interests and concerns | What we need | Power (1-5) | Interest (1-5) | Current stance | Required stance | Evidence |

## Power/Interest Grid
- Manage closely: ...
- Keep satisfied: ...
- Keep informed: ...
- Monitor: ...

## Priority commitment gaps
Top 3-5 stakeholders with the largest gap weighted by power.

## Engagement Plan
| Stakeholder | Objective | Key message | Owner | Channel | Cadence | Progress signal |

## Review schedule and open questions
```

## Constraints and limitations

- Ratings are judgements; record evidence and revisit them rather than treating the map as fact.
- The map is sensitive. Labelling a named person "opposed" in a widely shared document can damage relationships; keep it within the core team.
- Power is contextual; a junior specialist can hold decisive power over a technical approval.
- Avoid assumptions based on personal characteristics; base stance on observed behaviour and stated positions.

## Examples

**Initiative:** consolidating three regional customer support tools into one.
**Result highlights:** the CFO is high power, low interest (keep satisfied with a one-page cost summary each month). Regional support leads are high interest and medium power, currently passively opposed because of reporting-line fears; the engagement owner is the COO, who meets them individually before any announcement. The security team is a gatekeeper with high power over timing; the engagement objective is early architecture review, not persuasion. Frontline agents are high interest, low power: keep informed via a feedback channel and include representatives in tool testing.
