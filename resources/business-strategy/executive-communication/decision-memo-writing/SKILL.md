# Decision Memo Writing

A decision memo exists to get a good decision made quickly. The reader should know within the first paragraph what is being decided, by whom, by when, and what the author recommends. The rest of the memo earns trust by showing the alternatives fairly and making the reasoning auditable.

## When to use

- Requesting approval for an investment, policy, hire, launch, vendor, or strategic choice.
- When a decision has been discussed in meetings without closure.
- To record the reasoning behind a significant decision for later review.
- Not for status updates or information-only reports; those have no decision to make.

## Inputs

- The decision to be made, the decision-maker, and the deadline.
- Background facts and data, with sources.
- Options under consideration, including any already rejected.
- Stakeholder positions, including disagreements.
- Constraints (budget, legal, timing) and the criteria the decision-maker cares about.

## Procedure

1. **Write the decision line first.** One sentence: "Decision needed: whether to X, by <date>, from <approver>." Follow with the recommendation in one sentence. If you cannot write these two sentences, the decision is not yet well framed; go back to the requester.
2. **Classify reversibility.** Is this a two-way door (cheap to reverse, so decide fast with less analysis) or a one-way door (costly or impossible to reverse, so warrants more evidence and broader consultation)? This framing is popularised by Amazon's shareholder letters. Say which, and match the depth of the memo to it.
3. **Give only the context needed.** Three to six sentences on why this decision arises now and what happens if no decision is made. Move history and detail to an appendix.
4. **State criteria before options.** List the three to five criteria (for example cost, time to impact, risk, strategic fit, customer impact), with weights if they are not equal. Agreeing criteria first prevents the debate from becoming a contest of preferences.
5. **Present genuine options.** At least two real options plus "do nothing / defer" where relevant. For each: description, how it performs on each criterion, main risk, cost and timeline. Write each option as its strongest advocate would.
6. **Make the recommendation and argue it.** Explain why the recommended option wins on the criteria and what is given up. Quantify where possible and cite sources.
7. **Record risks and mitigations.** Top three risks of the recommended option, with likelihood, impact, and mitigation.
8. **Include dissent.** Name the strongest counter-argument and, if stakeholders disagree, summarise their position fairly (with their agreement on the wording when possible). Memos that hide dissent lose credibility when it surfaces.
9. **State what would change the recommendation.** List the assumptions or evidence that, if different, would flip the recommendation. Add check-in or kill criteria for after the decision.
10. **Edit hard.** Aim for one to two pages in the main body. Use plain language, active voice, specific numbers, and no unexplained acronyms. Check that the first paragraph stands alone.

## Context
## Decision criteria
| Criterion | Weight | Why it matters |
## Options
| | Option A | Option B | Do nothing |
|---|---|---|---|

## Recommendation and rationale

## Constraints and limitations

- The memo can be no better than its inputs; mark unsupported claims and assumptions.
- Weighted criteria can create false precision; use them to structure debate, not to compute the answer mechanically.
- The decision belongs to the named decision-maker; the memo should not read as if the decision is already made.

## Examples

**Request:** "Write a memo asking the COO to approve moving tier-1 support to an outsourced provider."
**Strong opening:** "Decision needed: whether to outsource tier-1 support to Provider X from Q2, by 15 January, from the COO. Recommendation: approve a six-month pilot covering email tickets only, which is a two-way door with a 60-day exit clause, rather than a full transfer, which is close to a one-way door because it requires redeploying 18 agents."
The memo then compares full outsourcing, pilot, and hiring internally against cost per ticket, customer satisfaction risk, time to scale, and reversibility, and records the support lead's objection about knowledge loss with a mitigation.
