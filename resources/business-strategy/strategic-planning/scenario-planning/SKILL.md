# Scenario Planning

Scenario planning replaces a single forecast with several internally consistent stories about how the external environment could evolve. The aim is better decisions under uncertainty: strategies that hold up across futures, contingent moves prepared in advance, and early signals that tell you which way things are moving.

## When to use

- A decision has a horizon of roughly two years or more, or involves large irreversible commitments.
- Experts disagree about key external drivers, or past forecasts in the area have been unreliable.
- Leadership is anchored on one expected future and needs it challenged.
- Not for operational forecasting with good historical data; use statistical forecasting there. Not for internal choices the organisation controls; those are options, not uncertainties.

## Inputs

- The focal question: the decision or strategy to test, with its time horizon ("Should we build our own EU data centre capacity by 2030?").
- Background material on market, technology, regulatory, economic, social, and competitive trends.
- The current strategy or the options under consideration.
- Optionally, views from people inside and outside the organisation.

## Procedure

1. **Sharpen the focal question.** Define the decision, horizon, and geography. A vague question ("the future of retail") produces vague scenarios.
2. **List driving forces.** Use a PESTLE-style sweep (political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental) plus industry forces. Aim for 15-30 forces, each phrased neutrally ("pace of adoption of X", not "X will boom").
3. **Separate predetermined elements from uncertainties.** Predetermined elements are forces whose direction is fairly clear (demographic shifts already in motion, signed regulations). They appear in every scenario. The rest are uncertainties.
4. **Rank uncertainties.** Rate each on impact on the focal question and degree of uncertainty. Pick the two that are highest on both and are reasonably independent of each other; correlated axes produce two near-identical quadrants.
5. **Define the axes.** Give each axis two clearly described end states ("regulation: fragmented national rules" vs. "harmonised regional regime"). Extremes should be plausible, not absurd.
6. **Build four scenarios.** For each quadrant write a name that is memorable, a short narrative of how the world got there from today, the conditions at the horizon, and the implications for customers, competitors, and the organisation. Include predetermined elements in all four. Check internal consistency: would these conditions coexist?
7. **Test the strategy (wind-tunnelling).** For each strategy or option, assess performance in each scenario (strong, adequate, weak, fails). Classify moves as robust (good in all), contingent (good in some; prepare triggers), or bets (good in one; take only knowingly).
8. **Define signposts.** For each scenario, list two to four observable indicators that would signal movement toward it, with a threshold and owner for monitoring. Signposts should be leading, measurable, and checked on a set cadence.
9. **Record decisions.** Summarise no-regret moves to make now, options to keep open, and triggers for contingent actions.

## Constraints and limitations

- Do not attach precise probabilities to scenarios unless there is a real basis; the method's value is in exploring plausibility.
- Avoid a "good, bad, base case" set built on one variable; that is sensitivity analysis, not scenario planning.
- Two axes simplify reality; important uncertainties not chosen as axes should still appear in narratives where relevant.
- Scenarios built only from internal opinion can share blind spots; include outside perspectives where possible.

## Examples

**Focal question:** Should a regional logistics firm invest in an electric delivery fleet by 2030?
**Axes chosen:** pace of charging-infrastructure build-out (slow vs. fast) and urban access regulation for combustion vehicles (permissive vs. restrictive).
**Scenarios:** "Plug-in Patchwork" (fast build-out, permissive rules), "Green Gates" (fast, restrictive), "Stranded Mandate" (slow, restrictive), "Diesel Drift" (slow, permissive).
**Stress-test finding:** leasing electric vans for dense urban routes is robust; buying a full fleet outright is a bet on "Green Gates". Signposts include city low-emission-zone announcements and depot grid-connection lead times.
