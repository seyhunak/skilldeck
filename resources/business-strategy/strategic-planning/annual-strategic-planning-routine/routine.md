# Annual Strategic Planning Routine

## Objective
Run a once-a-year planning cycle that refreshes the organisation's strategy from evidence, makes explicit choices about where to focus and what to stop, and then translates those choices into priorities, targets, OKRs, and a budget that fund them. The output is an approved strategy kernel, a prioritised and resourced initiative portfolio, company-level targets and OKRs, and a communicated plan. The routine keeps strategy ahead of numbers so that the budget expresses the strategy rather than replacing it.

## Trigger
Start roughly 12-16 weeks before the fiscal year begins, so that the budget can be approved before year start. Also trigger an out-of-cycle run after a major shock (loss of a key customer segment, regulation change, merger). The routine is coordinated by a strategy or planning lead and executed by people; an assistant can support analyses and drafts at each phase but does not run the process.

## Prerequisites
- Executive sponsor (usually the CEO) and a planning lead with time allocated.
- Planning calendar agreed with leadership, finance, and, where applicable, board meeting dates.
- Access to last year's strategy, performance data, QBR outputs, customer and market evidence.
- Finance's preliminary view of the cost base and funding capacity for next year.

## Inputs
- Prior-year strategy and how results compared with it (from quarterly business reviews).
- Market, customer, competitor, technology, and regulatory evidence.
- Functional self-assessments of capabilities and constraints.
- Financial baseline: run-rate revenue and costs, committed spend, capital constraints.
- Board or owner expectations and risk appetite.

## Steps
1. **Kick off and set the frame (weeks 1-2).** Confirm scope, calendar, roles, and the planning assumptions finance will use (macroeconomic, currency, inflation). Publish the timeline and the template each function must use.
2. **Build the fact base (weeks 2-4).** Review last year's performance against strategy: what worked, what did not, and why. Refresh the external view with `industry-structure-analysis` and recent market and competitor evidence; summarise internal capabilities. Keep the fact base short and sourced.
3. **Refresh the diagnosis and choices (weeks 4-6).** In a leadership workshop, test whether last year's diagnosis of the critical challenge still holds. Update the guiding policy and the explicit "will not do" list. Use the Rumelt kernel approach supported by `strategic-planning-agent` if a fresh draft is needed.
4. **Stress-test against scenarios (week 6).** Use `scenario-planning` to check the chosen direction against three or four plausible futures. Identify no-regret moves, contingent moves with triggers, and signposts to review at each QBR.
5. **Generate and prioritise initiatives (weeks 6-8).** Functions propose initiatives that implement the choices, each with objective, cost, benefit hypothesis, and dependencies. Evaluate them against agreed criteria (consider `strategy-options-evaluation-prompt`). Fund a short list; explicitly stop or defer others, including existing work that no longer fits.
6. **Set targets and the metric tree (weeks 8-9).** Define company-level outcome targets and connect them to driver metrics with `kpi-tree-design` so each function sees which levers it owns. Check targets against capacity and the funded initiatives; ambition without funding is a gap to resolve, not a stretch goal.
7. **Draft company OKRs and cascade (weeks 9-10).** Write three to five company objectives; teams draft aligned OKRs (with support from `okr-design-agent`) for the first quarter or half, not the full year in detail.
8. **Align the budget (weeks 9-12).** Finance builds the budget bottom-up from the funded initiatives and run costs. Reconcile top-down targets and bottom-up costs; trade-offs are decided by leadership, not by uniform percentage cuts.
9. **Approve (weeks 12-13).** Leadership signs off the strategy, portfolio, targets, and budget; the board approves where required.
10. **Communicate and embed (weeks 13-16).** Publish a concise strategy narrative for all staff, cascade through managers using `stakeholder-mapping` to plan who needs which message, and schedule QBR checkpoints that review signposts, initiatives, and OKRs.

## Decision points
- If the fact base shows the prior diagnosis is wrong or outdated, allocate an extra workshop before initiative planning rather than proceeding with old choices.
- If total proposed initiative cost exceeds funding capacity by a wide margin, return to prioritisation instead of trimming each initiative proportionally.
- If scenario testing shows the strategy works in only one future, either add hedging moves or explicitly accept the bet with leadership sign-off.
- If targets cannot be connected to driver metrics with owners, treat them as aspirations and do not use them for incentive purposes until resolved.
- If the timetable slips, protect the diagnosis and prioritisation steps; compress communication preparation instead.

## Expected output
- Fact base summary (performance review, external analysis, capability view).
- Strategy kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, "will not do" list, coherent actions.
- Scenario stress-test results and signposts.
- Prioritised initiative portfolio with funded, deferred, and stopped lists.
- KPI tree with company targets and owners; company OKRs and first-period team OKRs.
- Approved budget reconciled to the portfolio.
- Communication pack and QBR review calendar.

## Validation checklist
- [ ] The diagnosis cites evidence from the fact base, not only leadership opinion.
- [ ] The "will not do" list names real activities that are being stopped or declined.
- [ ] Every funded initiative traces to a strategic choice and has an owner and budget line.
- [ ] Company targets connect to driver metrics with named owners.
- [ ] The budget reconciles to the initiative portfolio and run-rate costs.
- [ ] Signposts from the scenario work are scheduled for review at each QBR.
- [ ] Approval decisions and dates are recorded.

## Human approval
The executive team owns the strategy and the prioritisation; the CEO approves the final plan and the board approves strategy and budget where its governance requires. Finance approves budget figures. Headcount and organisational changes that arise from the plan follow HR and legal processes before any announcement. An assistant may prepare analyses and drafts at every step, but no plan, target, or budget is final until approved by the accountable people, and nothing is communicated to staff before leadership sign-off.
