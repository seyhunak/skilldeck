# Industry Structure Analysis

Michael Porter's Five Forces framework explains the average profitability of an industry by the structure of competition around it: rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants, bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers, and threat of substitutes. Porter's own guidance stresses that the forces should be analysed with evidence about underlying structure, not with adjectives, and that the purpose is to find positions where the forces are weakest.

## When to use

- Evaluating whether to enter, expand in, or exit an industry or segment.
- Building the external part of a strategy diagnosis.
- Explaining margin pressure or a shift in industry profitability.
- Not for profiling individual competitors' features or tactics; use a competitor analysis for that. Complementors and regulation are not forces but factors that shape them.

## Inputs

- The industry and, ideally, the company whose position is being assessed.
- Evidence: market reports, company filings, pricing information, customer and supplier lists, switching-cost information, regulatory documents, expert notes.
- The geographic scope and time frame of interest.

## Procedure

1. **Define the industry.** Specify product scope and geographic scope. Test the boundary: if two segments have different buyers, suppliers, or entry barriers, analyse them separately. A wrong boundary invalidates everything that follows.
2. **Establish profitability context.** Where data exists, note the industry's average returns or margins over several years, and how they compare with other industries. If unavailable, say so; the analysis then explains structure without verifying it against returns.
3. **Assess rivalry.** Evidence to look for: number and relative size of competitors, industry growth rate, product differentiation, fixed-cost intensity and capacity additions, exit barriers, and whether competition is on price or on other dimensions. Price competition is the most damaging form.
4. **Assess threat of entry.** Barriers: supply-side economies of scale, demand-side network effects, customer switching costs, capital requirements, incumbency advantages independent of size (proprietary technology, locations, brand), access to distribution, restrictive regulation, and expected retaliation. Cite recent entrants and how they fared.
5. **Assess buyer power.** Buyer concentration and purchase volume, product standardisation, switching costs, threat of backward integration, price sensitivity (share of the buyer's costs, effect on the buyer's own quality). Distinguish buyer segments; power often differs sharply between them.
6. **Assess supplier power.** Supplier concentration relative to the industry, dependence of suppliers on this industry, switching costs, differentiation of inputs, availability of substitutes for inputs, and threat of forward integration. Include labour if specialised talent is scarce.
7. **Assess threat of substitutes.** Products that perform the same function by different means, including doing without or doing it in-house. Evaluate relative price-performance and the buyer's cost of switching to the substitute.
8. **Rate each force.** Use low, medium, or high with a one-sentence justification and an evidence-quality tag (strong: data or filings; moderate: credible reports; weak: inference). Avoid averaging forces mechanically; one very strong force can cap industry profitability.
9. **Identify shifts.** Note factors changing the forces: technology, regulation, growth changes, consolidation, complementors. State the direction and approximate timing of each shift.
10. **Derive implications.** For the company: where to position to avoid the strongest forces, how to reshape forces in its favour (raise switching costs, standardise supplier inputs, build entry barriers), and which segments are structurally most attractive.

## Constraints and limitations

- The framework describes industry-level structure; it explains averages, not why one firm outperforms another.
- Fast-changing industries require repeated analysis; ratings are snapshots.
- Public data on margins, shares, and switching costs is often incomplete; label inferences and never invent figures.
- It is weaker for analysing platform ecosystems with strong complementors; add a complementor analysis there.

## Examples

**Industry:** independent veterinary clinics in a mid-sized national market.
**Highlights:** rivalry medium (fragmented, local differentiation through relationships); threat of entry medium (low capital for a single clinic but scarce vets); supplier power high for veterinary labour and concentrated pharmaceutical distribution; buyer power low (individual pet owners, emotional purchase, though price comparison is rising); substitutes low-to-medium (online pharmacies for medication). Shift: corporate consolidators acquiring clinics raises rivalry for talent. Implication: the scarce resource is clinical staff, so strategy should focus on becoming the preferred employer.
