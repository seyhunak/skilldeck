# Unit Economics Analysis

Unit economics measure the profit generated by one unit — usually one customer or one order — over its life, compared with the cost of acquiring it. The essential discipline is consistent definitions: most bad conclusions come from mixing revenue and margin, periods, or cohorts.

## When to use

- Deciding whether to increase acquisition spend or which channels to scale.
- Checking that a pricing or packaging change improves economics, not just conversion.
- Preparing an investor or board view of growth efficiency.
- Supporting a business case whose benefits come from customer revenue.
- Not for valuing a whole company; use a discounted cash flow valuation for that.

## Inputs

- Period-level sales and marketing costs, ideally by channel (paid media, content, sales salaries and commissions, tools, agencies).
- New customers acquired per period and channel.
- Revenue per customer (ARPA / ARPU) and its distribution by segment or plan.
- Variable costs per customer: hosting or COGS, payment fees, support, onboarding, account management.
- Retention data: monthly or annual logo churn and revenue churn, ideally by cohort; expansion revenue.
- Business model: subscription, transactional, or marketplace (definitions differ).

## Procedure

1. **Fix the unit and the period.** Choose the unit (customer, account, order) and keep monthly or annual figures consistent throughout. Write each definition at the top of the output.
2. **Compute CAC.** CAC = fully loaded sales and marketing cost to acquire customers in a period / new customers acquired in that period. Report blended CAC and paid-only CAC separately, and by channel where costs can be attributed. Consider a lag between spend and acquisition for long sales cycles (for example attributing spend from the previous quarter). Exclude costs for retaining existing customers, or state that they are included.
3. **Compute contribution margin per customer.** Contribution per period = ARPA minus variable costs to serve (COGS, payment fees, variable support). Gross margin percentage = contribution / ARPA. Use margin, not revenue, in all lifetime calculations.
4. **Estimate lifetime.** For subscriptions with roughly stable churn, expected lifetime is approximately 1 / churn rate per period (for example 2% monthly churn implies about 50 months). Where revenue expansion is material, use net revenue retention or cohort curves instead. Cap lifetimes at a sensible horizon (often three to five years) when churn data is immature, and say so.
5. **Compute LTV.** Simple LTV = contribution per period x expected lifetime in periods = (ARPA x gross margin %) / churn rate. Optionally discount future margin. Present LTV by segment; blended LTV hides segments with very different churn.
6. **Compute ratios.** LTV:CAC = LTV / CAC. CAC payback (months) = CAC / monthly contribution per customer. A widely quoted heuristic for venture-backed SaaS is LTV:CAC of around 3 or more and payback within 12-18 months; these are rules of thumb, vary by stage and model, and are not targets on their own.
7. **Segment and compare.** Build a table by channel and by segment or plan. Look for channels with acceptable blended averages but poor marginal economics, and for segments that are cheap to acquire but churn fast.
8. **Sanity-check.** Confirm that lifetimes implied by churn are consistent with observed cohort ages; that CAC includes salaries, not just media; that LTV uses margin; and that marketplace or transactional models use per-order or per-buyer frequency instead of subscription churn.
9. **Run sensitivities.** Show how LTV:CAC and payback change with plus or minus 20% on churn, ARPA, and CAC, and state which assumption matters most.

## Constraints and limitations

- Early-stage companies often lack enough churn history; LTV then rests heavily on assumptions and should be shown as a range.
- Attribution of CAC to channels is imperfect; multi-touch journeys blur channel economics.
- Averages conceal distributions; a few large customers can dominate ARPA.
- These figures support internal decisions and are not financial or investment advice.

## Examples

**Input:** paid CAC 1,200, ARPA 150/month, gross margin 75%, monthly logo churn 2.5%.
**Calculation:** monthly contribution = 150 x 0.75 = 112.50; lifetime = 1 / 0.025 = 40 months; LTV = 112.50 x 40 = 4,500; LTV:CAC = 3.75; payback = 1,200 / 112.50 = about 10.7 months.
**Interpretation:** healthy on paper, but if the churn figure comes from cohorts younger than 12 months the 40-month lifetime is an extrapolation; show LTV with a 24-month cap as a conservative case (2,700, LTV:CAC 2.25).
