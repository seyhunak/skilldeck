# Plain Language Rewriting

Plain language is not dumbing down. It is writing so that the intended audience can find what they need, understand it, and use it — the working definition used by the International Plain Language Federation and reflected in ISO 24495-1. The craft is in keeping every fact, condition, and obligation while removing the friction around them.

## When to use

- Customer-facing notices, letters, terms summaries, and help content.
- Internal policies, announcements, and procedures that staff skim.
- Technical explanations for non-technical stakeholders.
- Emails and documents that keep generating clarification questions.

Not appropriate when the exact wording is legally or regulatorily operative (contract clauses, statutory notices, regulated disclosures). In those cases produce a plain-language companion summary and leave the operative text unchanged unless a qualified reviewer approves edits.

## Inputs

- The original text. Required.
- The reader: who they are, what they know, what they need to do after reading. Required; ask if not given.
- The channel (email, web page, letter, mobile) and any length limits.
- Terms that must not change (product names, defined legal terms, regulated phrases).

## Procedure

1. **Identify the reader's task.** Write one sentence: "After reading, <reader> needs to <action/understanding>." Everything in the rewrite serves that sentence.
2. **Extract the content inventory.** List every fact, number, date, condition, exception, obligation, and consequence in the original. This list is the contract for the rewrite: nothing on it may be lost or changed.
3. **Reorder for the reader.** Put the most important information first: what is happening, what the reader must do, by when. Background and rationale go after. Group related points under descriptive headings phrased as the reader's questions where helpful ("What do I need to do?").
4. **Rewrite sentences.** Aim for an average around 15 to 20 words, one main idea per sentence. Use active voice with a clear actor ("We will refund you" not "A refund will be issued"). Address the reader as "you" and the organisation as "we". Turn nominalisations back into verbs ("decide" not "make a decision"; "apply" not "submit an application").
5. **Choose common words.** Replace jargon and formal words with everyday equivalents (use, start, help, about, because, if). Keep a technical term only if the reader needs it, and define it on first use. Expand acronyms once or remove them.
6. **Use structure.** Numbered lists for steps in order, bullets for unordered items, tables for comparisons, and short paragraphs. Put conditions before consequences ("If you miss the deadline, your claim will close").
7. **Handle obligations and qualifiers precisely.** Use "must" for requirements and "should" for recommendations; never soften a requirement. Keep every "unless", "only if", and exception from the inventory, ideally in its own sentence.
8. **Check against the inventory.** Tick every item from step 2 in the rewrite. Note anything removed deliberately (true repetition, internal process detail the reader does not need) and why.
9. **Test readability.** Read it as the target reader. Optionally compute a readability score (for example Flesch-Kincaid grade) as a rough signal only; formulas measure word and sentence length, not comprehension. Where possible, recommend a quick test with a real reader: can they say what they need to do?

## Expected output

1. The rewritten text.
2. A change summary: main structural changes and any terms defined.
3. A meaning-preservation table for anything sensitive:

| Original element | Where it is in the rewrite | Note |
|---|---|---|

4. Items needing confirmation, such as ambiguities in the original that the rewrite had to resolve.

## Constraints and limitations

- Never change meaning, obligations, numbers, dates, or legal effect. If the original is ambiguous, ask or flag; do not pick a reading silently.
- Do not remove required disclosures, warnings, or regulatory phrases.
- Readability formulas are crude; a low grade level does not guarantee clarity.
- Translation and localisation are separate tasks; plain English helps translation but is not a substitute.
- Respect the organisation's style guide and mandated terms.

## Examples

Original:
> "In the event that the account holder fails to remit payment in full prior to the expiration of the 30-day period subsequent to the invoice date, the organisation reserves the right to effect suspension of services without further notification."

Rewrite:
> **Pay within 30 days of the invoice date.** If you do not pay the full amount in that time, we may suspend your service without warning you again.

Preserved: 30 days, from invoice date, full payment, suspension is optional ("may"), no further notice. Changed: passive and nominalised phrasing to active verbs; deadline moved to the front.
